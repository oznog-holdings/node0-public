Anyone building a fleet operated by several automated actors meets this
question in the first week. Does each actor get its own operating-system
identity, or do they share one? On 20260811 Christoph chose to share, for
bring-up. Building per-role privilege boundaries before the fleet's real
shape was known would have slowed every deployment and baked in guesses
that would later have to be unpicked.

The trade-off was written down rather than hidden. Agents that share an
account share its authority, and one leaked key reaches whatever that
account reaches. It was accepted as a cost of bring-up, with narrowing it
on the open list.

What the decision bought was attribution without isolation. It holds
only because of a second rule that costs almost nothing to keep. No two
agents ever share a private key or an API token, even where they land in
the same account. Revocation is then per agent, and so is the record of
who did what. By the collection of 20260920 the fleet repository's 2,024
commits split cleanly by author field between two AI agents and one
human, which is the evidence that attribution works without
operating-system separation.

The larger lesson is that an agent is an identity, and deserves what a
person gets: an account, credentials of its own, a record, and a way to be
removed. That diligence is not always possible. On an operator's own
laptop, many agent sessions often run at once under the person's account,
and nothing at the operating-system level tells them apart. Something else has
to narrow what each one can do: its own keys, its own tokens, and the
limits of the tools it is handed.

Scale changes the question again. Onboarding a hundred people in a day is
rare; a hundred agents in a day may become ordinary. Accounts and keys made
by hand will not keep up, so identity for agents has to be issued by a
program: requested by the agent, scoped to the job, short-lived, and
logged. Node0's secrets vault is where that is headed.

The general form, separated from Node0's particulars: a small set of
automated operators stays attributable without per-role accounts if every
actor holds its own credentials and no two ever share one. It does not stay
contained that way. Containment comes from narrower identities, and once
agents arrive by the dozen, only a program can issue those.
