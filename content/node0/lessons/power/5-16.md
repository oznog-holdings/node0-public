Before any active cooling, the server room sat at about 90 degrees
Fahrenheit and there was no way to bring it lower. The planned answer was a
dedicated mini-split, already sized and quoted, scheduled for 2027. The
answer that actually happened on 20260827 was to redirect a branch of
cooling capacity that already existed in the building into the room, which
held it around 78 degrees and cost nothing in hardware.

That part is a good story but not the lesson. The lesson is what was done
next, which was to keep treating the fix as provisional.

The numbers that justified the interim approach were projections for a fleet
that is not yet fully built out, so the redirect might be adequate today and
inadequate at full load without anything visibly changing. Two habits keep
that from becoming an under-provisioned permanent arrangement. The first is
a deliberate week of pure observation before trusting the result, rather
than declaring victory when the temperature first read 78. The second is
keeping the mini-split sized, priced and live as a fallback rather than
quietly cancelling it once the cheap fix appeared to work.

There is also a measurement trick here worth stealing. Heat load in a server
room is very close to electrical load, and Node0 already polls UPS output
wattage every scrape interval for entirely different reasons. Converting
that to BTU gives a continuously measured heat load using an instrument
already in place, with no new sensor and no estimate. If you are solving a
thermal problem on a budget, look first for an instrument you already own,
and treat "it seems to be holding" as a hypothesis you keep testing rather
than a conclusion you file.
